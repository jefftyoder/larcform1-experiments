## Abstract (condensed)

Arctic amplification appears in past warm and glacial periods, historical
observations, and climate model experiments. Temperature, water vapour, and cloud
feedbacks have all been proposed as contributors, but the surface albedo feedback
(increased solar absorption as snow/ice retreat) is often cited as the main
contributor — yet amplification also occurs in models with snow and ice cover held
fixed. Analysing CMIP5 simulations to quantify each feedback's contribution, the
authors find the largest contribution to Arctic amplification comes from
**temperature feedbacks**: as the surface warms, more energy is radiated back to
space in low latitudes than in the Arctic. This arises from (i) the different
vertical structure of warming at high vs low latitudes and (ii) the smaller
increase in emitted blackbody radiation per unit warming at colder temperatures.
The **surface albedo feedback is the second main contributor**; other suggested
contributions are substantially smaller or even oppose Arctic amplification.

## Main text (condensed)

### Background and framing

- Quantitative understanding of Arctic amplification mechanisms is key to
  confidence in projections and to targeting model–data comparisons. The surface
  albedo feedback is well established as a partial cause; although IPCC AR4 stated
  the main cause was unclear, many recent studies indicate or assume surface
  albedo is dominant. Yet amplification occurs in models *without* surface albedo
  feedback, and amplification in coupled models has been shown to be primarily
  caused by feedbacks acting on terrestrial longwave radiation — implying albedo
  (a shortwave feedback) can play only a secondary role.
- Other proposed contributors: water vapour feedback (greenhouse effect of added
  water vapour), cloud feedback (changed cloud effect on the radiative balance),
  the lapse-rate feedback tied to the vertical structure of warming, and changes
  in atmospheric and oceanic heat transport.

### Lapse-rate and Planck feedbacks

- The TOA temperature feedback decomposes into vertically uniform warming (Planck
  feedback) plus deviations from vertical uniformity (lapse-rate feedback).
- **Tropics:** deep convection tightly couples surface and upper troposphere;
  warming releases more latent heat, steepening the moist adiabat and warming the
  upper troposphere more than the surface. Under this top-heavy profile a smaller
  surface temperature increase offsets a given TOA imbalance → lapse-rate feedback
  negative.
- **Arctic:** cold dense air near the surface hardly mixes with lighter air aloft;
  radiation is the primary coupling. Warming remains confined to the lowermost
  atmosphere (bottom-heavy profile), so a larger surface increase is needed to
  offset a given TOA imbalance → lapse-rate feedback positive.
- **Planck feedback:** longwave emission follows `R = ε σ T⁴` (ε ≈ 1, σ the
  Stefan–Boltzmann constant), so a given increase in emitted radiation requires a
  larger temperature increase at colder background temperature. Example: at 30 °C
  an external forcing of 1 W m⁻² is balanced by 0.16 °C of warming, whereas at
  −30 °C, 0.31 °C is required. Because the Arctic is colder than the tropics, the
  Planck feedback in itself causes Arctic amplification.

### Feedback decomposition (TOA perspective)

- Individual contributions to Arctic amplification are assessed as the difference
  between contributions to Arctic and tropical warming (Figure 2), using inverted
  radiative kernels to convert flux changes into warming contributions (Methods).
- CMIP5 4 × CO₂ minus control (last 30 years each): intermodel mean warming is
  **11.2 K in the Arctic (60°N–90°N)** and **4.3 K in the tropics (30°S–30°N)**
  (Figure 1). Arctic warming is strongest in winter (DJF, 15.9 K) and weakest in
  summer (JJA, 6.5 K).
- Conventional TOA decomposition (Figure 2a): the largest contributor to Arctic
  amplification is the **lapse-rate feedback**, followed by **surface albedo** and
  **Planck** feedbacks. In absolute terms the albedo feedback contributes slightly
  more to Arctic *warming*, but the lapse-rate feedback also reduces tropical
  warming, giving it the greater contribution to *amplification* (distance from
  the 1:1 line). Water vapour feedback and CO₂ radiative forcing both warm the
  tropics more, opposing Arctic amplification.
- **Fixed-relative-humidity framework** (warming at constant RH as one feedback
  plus a small RH-change feedback): assigns a slightly larger amplification
  contribution to the alternative lapse-rate feedback (Arctic: +3.8 K; tropics:
  −2.2 K) than to the surface albedo feedback (Arctic: +5.7 K); the alternative
  Planck feedback's effect on amplification is close to zero. In this framework
  the temperature–moisture and surface albedo feedbacks are thus of roughly equal
  importance to Arctic amplification.

### Seasonal cycle

- Winter (DJF) warming exceeds summer (JJA) warming (Figure 1b), linked to winter
  release of heat stored in the ocean during summer and increased downwelling
  longwave radiation.
- From a TOA perspective (Figure 2b): surface albedo and water vapour feedbacks
  favour stronger *summer* warming but are outweighed by seasonal ocean heat
  storage and the lapse-rate feedback. Seasonal heat storage (including latent
  heat of melting sea ice) mitigates about two-thirds of the summertime effect of
  surface albedo change; the heat is released in winter, which together with the
  positive lapse-rate feedback yields winter-amplified Arctic warming. The Arctic
  lapse-rate feedback is much weaker in summer than winter — actually slightly
  negative in summer.

### Surface perspective

- The TOA-based decomposition is internally consistent but physically
  unsatisfying: the Arctic lapse-rate feedback reflects the breakdown of a
  vertical-coupling assumption rather than a specific physical mechanism.
  Analysing feedbacks at the surface in addition to the TOA (Figure 2c) clarifies
  what causes the surface amplification reflected in the lapse-rate feedback.
- At the surface, the temperature feedback splits into a negative **surface
  temperature feedback** (longwave emitted from the surface) and a positive
  **atmospheric warming feedback** (downwelling longwave received by the surface).
- The largest contribution to Arctic amplification arises from the **surface
  temperature feedback**, due to the smaller increase in longwave emission per
  unit warming at colder temperatures. This nonlinear blackbody dependence matters
  more from a surface than a TOA perspective because the meridional temperature
  gradient is larger at the surface than in the troposphere.
- The **atmospheric temperature feedback** contributes to amplification because
  the near-surface atmosphere warms more in the Arctic than the tropics. Earlier
  surface-perspective studies implicitly included the spatial structure of the
  temperature feedback and so missed the key role of the surface temperature
  feedback's structure.
- **Clouds:** in the annual mean, cloud feedback opposes Arctic amplification from
  a TOA perspective but makes a small positive contribution from a surface
  perspective. Within the lowest 1–2 km of the Arctic atmosphere, cloud-top
  temperatures are often similar to surface temperatures, so low-level clouds
  hardly affect TOA longwave fluxes (clouds radiate upward at roughly the surface
  temperature) but increase downward longwave, warming the surface at the expense
  of the atmosphere. Increased/thickened low cloud in a warming climate thus
  barely affects TOA cloud feedback but yields a positive surface cloud feedback.
  Likewise, water vapour feedback contributes more to summer than winter warming
  from a TOA perspective, but more strongly to surface warming in winter than
  summer (not shown).

### Intermodel spread and transports

- Intermodel spread in Arctic warming is dominated by spread in **local feedback
  mechanisms**, not meridional transport changes (Figure 3). Atmospheric
  heat-transport changes *dampen* intermodel spread, being more positive in models
  with little Arctic warming — consistent with energy-balance-model
  reconstructions of warming and transport in CMIP3.
- In the ensemble mean, atmospheric heat transport does contribute to Arctic
  amplification (enhancing Arctic, reducing tropical warming; Figure 2a). Contrary
  to physical intuition, poleward atmospheric energy transport does not scale with
  the meridional temperature gradient within individual models: it increases in
  most models despite a reduced Equator-to-pole temperature gradient, because
  increasing latent energy transport overcompensates the decrease of dry static
  energy transport. Changes in ocean transport and ocean heat uptake are not
  correlated with total Arctic warming across models.

### Conclusion

Contrary to a widespread assumption, **temperature feedbacks are the most
important contributors to Arctic amplification in contemporary climate models**.
The surface albedo feedback is the second main contributor; other suggested
drivers either play minor roles or oppose Arctic amplification in the ensemble
mean.

## Methods (condensed; equations verbatim)

Previous feedback diagnoses based on TOA and surface fluxes precisely assess
longwave/shortwave flux changes but cannot quantify the temperature changes
associated with individual feedbacks. Here the **radiative kernel technique** is
used and extended (inverted) to compute the local warming contributions of the
feedbacks.

A radiative kernel kᵢ is the change in TOA radiation ΔRᵢ caused by a small change
in climate variable xᵢ (e.g. a one per cent change in surface albedo, dxᵢ):
kᵢ = dR/dxᵢ. The TOA flux change caused by one feedback in a climate-change
experiment is estimated as ΔRᵢ = kᵢ · Δxᵢ, where Δxᵢ is for instance the surface
albedo change between control and perturbed climate. Flux changes are converted
into temperature responses per feedback.

The warming response to a TOA flux imbalance is decomposed into three components:
a global mean Planck feedback, the local deviation from the global mean Planck
feedback, and the effect of the lapse-rate feedback (deviations from vertically
uniform warming), on surface temperature:

```
ΔT = Σᵢ ( ΔRᵢ ( d̄T/dR + dT′/dR + dT^LR/dR ) )
```

The warming contribution, for example of the surface albedo feedback, is:

```
ΔTₐ = ΔRₐ ( d̄T/dR )
```

and the contribution of the Planck feedback's deviation from its global mean is:

```
ΔT_P = Σᵢ ( ΔRᵢ dT′/dR )
```

The local warming contribution of the lapse-rate feedback is:

```
ΔT_LR = Σᵢ ( ΔRᵢ dT^LR/dR )
```

The warming response to a unit flux imbalance is the inverse of the vertically
integrated temperature kernel, dT/dR = 1/∫ k_T dp, obtained by summing over the
surface temperature kernel and all levels of the tropospheric temperature kernel.
Averaging across latitudes and longitudes decomposes this into the mean inverted
kernel and a local deviation. To obtain the full warming response including the
effect of the lapse-rate feedback, each level's kernel is weighted by its warming
relative to surface warming when vertically integrating:

```
∫ k_{T,weighted} = k_{Ts} + ∫ ( k_T · (ΔTᵢ/ΔTₛ) dp )
```

In the surface-based feedback analysis, the inverted surface temperature kernel
alone computes the warming response; atmospheric temperature change is treated as
a feedback contributing to the surface flux imbalance. The surface temperature
response is separated into a global mean component and the local deviation
analogous to the Planck feedback:

```
ΔT = Σᵢ ( ΔR_{s,i} ( d̄Tₛ/dRₛ + dTₛ′/dRₛ ) )
```

Atmospheric heat convergence is computed as the difference between TOA and
surface fluxes, assuming no atmospheric heat storage on the experiment's
timescale. Changes in oceanic heat convergence and ocean heat uptake (non-zero on
the timescale considered) are computed as changes in total surface fluxes. To
separate tropospheric and stratospheric responses, a tropopause height of 100 hPa
in the tropics (30°S–30°N) is assumed, decreasing linearly with latitude to
300 hPa at the poles. Surface downward and upward shortwave fluxes are used to
compute the effective albedo. Monthly mean data from the last 30 years of the
CMIP5 pre-industrial control and 4 × CO₂ runs are averaged into monthly
climatologies for the feedback calculations. Radiative kernels were obtained from
the MPI-ESM-LR control climate; using kernels from the 4 × CO₂ runs leads to a
smaller role of the albedo feedback, and using kernels from other models leads to
larger residuals, but neither qualitatively changes the conclusions.
