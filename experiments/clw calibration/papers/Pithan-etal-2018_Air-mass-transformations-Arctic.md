## Abstract (condensed)

Pulses of warm, moist air from lower latitudes are the Arctic's main energy source
outside summer; they can cause substantial surface warming and trigger ice melt.
Transport in the opposite direction produces cold-air outbreaks, often associated with
cold extremes over continents and extreme surface heat fluxes and occasional polar lows
over oceans. Air masses crossing the strong Arctic-to-mid-latitude temperature gradient
are rapidly transformed — into colder/drier or warmer/moister air — by cloud, radiative,
and turbulent processes, especially in the boundary layer. Liquid→ice phase changes in
boundary-layer clouds are critical: liquid water determines the clouds' radiative
effects, while ice is crucial for subsequent cloud decay or dissipation — processes
poorly represented in weather and climate models. A better understanding of how air
masses are transformed on their way into and out of the Arctic is essential for
improved prediction of weather and climate in the Arctic and mid-latitudes.
Observational and modelling exercises should take an air-mass-following Lagrangian
approach.

## Introduction

- The general circulation as a global heat engine (Laliberté et al. 2015): air is heated
  and moistened in the tropics, carried poleward, cooled and dried at high latitudes
  before returning equatorward (Pauluis et al. 2008). This review addresses how air is
  transformed as it cools and dries entering the Arctic (northward branch) or warms and
  moistens on its way south, out of the Arctic.
- By controlling radiative heat loss and vertical temperature structure, air-mass
  transformations are also crucial for Arctic amplification of climate change — seen in
  observations, reconstructions of past climates, and climate model experiments.
- **Intrusions:** most heat and moisture import into the Arctic occurs as pulses of
  anomalously warm, moist air from lower latitudes. They occur throughout the year and
  matter for extreme Arctic weather and climate in all seasons. Warm, moist air
  intruding in winter radiatively cools (or in summer cools by turbulent fluxes toward
  the melting surface), forming low-level liquid clouds that warm the surface (the
  warming effect is absent in summer). Continued radiative cooling in wintertime
  intrusions leads to frozen precipitation that gradually dries the air; clouds glaciate
  or dissipate in the cold, dry final stage of the air-mass transformation.
- **Cold-air outbreaks:** cold, dry Arctic air transported southward. Over winter ocean,
  the ice-free surface releases large amounts of heat and moisture to the much colder
  air; clouds develop atop a progressively deepening convective boundary layer, often
  arranged in streets roughly along the wind, breaking up into cellular structures
  farther from the sea-ice edge. Over continents, cold-air outbreaks cause extreme cold
  weather and severe disruption in populated lower-latitude areas. The review focuses
  primarily on marine outbreaks, where the transformation is faster and more intense
  than over continents.
- Both intrusions and outbreaks typically occur when the prevailing large-scale wind has
  a meridional orientation, often with a blocking-like high-pressure system. Their
  frequency depends on the tropical atmosphere state and stratospheric dynamics; changes
  in frequency would affect mid- and high-latitude weather and climate.
- The observational record of Arctic weather/climate is short and contains large trends
  plus large internal variability. Models have problems with many processes specific to
  Arctic air-mass transformations. Ongoing/planned observational and modelling efforts
  offer unprecedented opportunities.
- The article condenses understanding of boundary-layer dynamics triggered by strong
  surface–atmosphere temperature contrasts in both transformation types, then reviews
  their roles in the global climate system, and closes with an outlook.

## Intrusions of warm, moist air into the Arctic

- Mid-latitude air penetrates the Arctic basin mostly in filamentary structures akin to
  atmospheric rivers (Fig. 1). Intrusions occur throughout the year, typically initiated
  by large-scale flow anomalies featuring a blocking-like high near the Arctic Circle
  with a low-pressure system to its west (Fig. 1); this dipole generates strong poleward
  flow injecting mid-latitude air into the Arctic basin. Injection can occur at any
  longitude, but in winter there is a marked preference for the North Atlantic and North
  Pacific, where mid-latitude marine air lies closest to the Arctic. Such intrusions
  occur roughly once a week on average and take about five days to cross the Arctic. In
  summer, intrusions commonly originate over the warm Siberian land mass.

- Over 60% of the total moisture flux into the Arctic occurs during events occupying
  only 10% of the time, and more than one-third of the total flux occurs during
  spatially and temporally coherent intrusion events. Intrusions cause strong, locally
  concentrated positive column-water-vapour anomalies, usually accompanied by clouds,
  leading to positive surface temperature anomalies exceeding 6 K over land or sea ice.
  The surface warming results mostly from increased downward terrestrial radiation and
  can limit sea-ice growth (reducing the ocean's conductive heat loss through the ice)
  or trigger earlier spring-melt onset. On longer time scales, nearly half of the
  1979–2011 sea-ice decline in the Barents–Kara Seas can be attributed to increased heat
  and moisture flux from lower latitudes. Individual events matter: an intrusion caused
  melt over more than 90% of the Greenland ice sheet surface in summer 2012, and warm
  Siberian air advected northward over the Arctic Ocean caused rapid sea-ice melt in
  August 2014.
- **Lagrangian view of the transformation** (inset in Fig. 1): the lowermost atmosphere
  cools rapidly until reaching saturation, and cloud droplets form. As liquid-water
  clouds are largely opaque to terrestrial radiation, the strongest cooling is displaced
  to cloud top (Fig. 1, (2)). Cloud-top cooling generates downward motion driving
  turbulence, which can extend to the surface or form a separate turbulent layer. Ice
  particles appear as the cloud continues to cool radiatively. Despite moisture losses
  to precipitating ice crystals and latent-heat release from condensation, this
  mixed-phase state is often very long-lived, as radiative cooling and cloud-top
  entrainment of moist air across the elevated inversion sustain the cloud. In the cold
  season, these coupled radiative, dynamical, and microphysical processes eventually
  consume most of the available moisture; the cloud glaciates and/or dissipates,
  leaving a state where radiative surface cooling forms a surface-based temperature
  inversion (Fig. 1, (3)).
- Arctic air can also form over high-latitude continents, where it reaches even colder
  temperatures than over the Arctic ocean. Besides radiative cooling, evaporative
  cooling by precipitation falling into dry layers contributes. Over Canada, Arctic air
  formation is often associated with cold-air damming in the lee of the Rocky Mountains.
- The interaction of small-scale processes in shallow layers with cold temperatures,
  weak turbulence, and rapidly responding surfaces is challenging for global weather and
  climate models and for smaller-scale cloud-resolving models. Models struggle to
  represent both the cloudy and cloud-free states and the transition between them in a
  Lagrangian transformation, and generate a range of different vertical structures and
  surface energy exchanges. Maintaining supercooled liquid water in the presence of ice
  particles — which ultimately determines the cloud radiative effect at the surface —
  is a particular challenge, less pronounced in models that separately compute the
  temporal evolution of cloud ice and liquid water.
- The liquid/ice partitioning is particularly sensitive to the numbers of cloud
  condensation nuclei (CCN) and ice-nucleating particles, which have few obvious local
  Arctic sources. Relatively weak precipitation and sedimentation rates are important
  for sustaining liquid water over several days. Ice-nucleating particles from ice
  crystals sublimating below cloud can be recycled and contribute to sustaining ice
  processes. Intrusions may also supply CCN and ice-nucleating particles, which are
  usually more abundant at lower latitudes; relatively aerosol-rich free-tropospheric
  air can be an important source, particularly for clouds extending into the inversion.
  Collectively representing these processes is essential for capturing the time-scale
  of the transformation and thereby the spatial extent of the cloudy state, which
  provides substantial energy to the surface and maintains a higher surface temperature.

## Fast warming and moistening in marine cold-air outbreaks

- Cold-air outbreaks occur when cold Arctic air is advected into much warmer regions by
  the large-scale flow, with the strongest events produced by a pressure dipole: high on
  the western side, low on the eastern side (Fig. 2). They most frequently occur in
  bands stretching from the North Pacific to western North America and from the North
  Atlantic towards Europe. Over the warm western Atlantic and Pacific currents, cold air
  advected off the continents is mostly transformed in baroclinic processes driving the
  storm tracks. Discussed here are the canonical, more vertically uniform outbreaks that
  occur over the ice-free or partly freezing seas in the Arctic and develop most
  frequently in the cold sectors of cyclones. The extent and impact region of an
  outbreak is set both by the synoptic-eddy scale and by the along-stream horizontal
  scale of the transformation, which is proportional to the temperature difference
  between the open-water surface and the cold-air mass and can extend to about
  500–1,000 km; colder outbreaks or those reaching warmer sea surface temperatures have
  larger horizontal extent.

- The ocean–cold-air temperature difference can exceed 30 K and generates surface heat
  and moisture fluxes of 500 W m⁻² close to the coastline or sea-ice edge. The
  associated boundary-layer instability results in intense shallow convection, clearly
  identifiable on satellite imagery as cloud streets close to the sea-ice edge and open
  cells farther downstream (Fig. 2). Cloud streets with boundary-layer roll circulations
  typically occupy the first several hundred kilometres downwind of the sea-ice edge or
  coastline; large-eddy simulations show the roll organization is triggered by
  surface-temperature heterogeneities in the marginal ice zone. The roll-to-open-cell
  transition is often accompanied by a shift from thin, elongated stratocumulus to
  deeper, dispersed cumulus clouds — traditionally explained as a shift towards a more
  convective boundary-layer state with increasing latent-heat release in clouds
  relative to surface heating. A recent study concluded that mixed-phase microphysical
  processes — in particular, precipitation enhancement due to cloud-ice formation —
  play an important role in the stratocumulus-to-cumulus transition.
- Intense snow showers occur at downstream coastlines; these convective snowstorms are
  well studied for some regions (e.g. the densely populated Great Lakes region) but
  less investigated for the Arctic coast. Marine cold-air outbreaks account for 60–80%
  of the ocean heat loss over the Norwegian and Irminger seas during the cold season,
  play a key role in deep-water formation and ocean vertical mixing, and strongly
  influence new sea-ice formation and the ice and ocean dynamics of the marginal ice
  zone.
- While strong winds are common during marine outbreaks, they are further enhanced when
  the flow organizes in orographic jets, low-level baroclinic jets, fronts, and polar
  lows; the enhanced wind speeds further intensify surface heat flux and ocean heat
  loss.
- Model representation of convection and clouds in marine outbreaks remains
  problematic: models generally simulate the transition from a well-mixed stratocumulus
  boundary layer to a partly decoupled cumulus layer, but tend to underestimate cloud
  liquid water and ice under these conditions, leading to underestimated solar
  radiation reflected by clouds in the cold sectors of cyclones. The lack of cloud
  water is possibly related to an underestimate of surface fluxes and vertical mixing
  in the presence of strong wind shear.

## Slow air-mass transformation over continents

- Continental cold-air outbreaks often occur in anticyclonic conditions advecting
  Arctic air southward from Canada or westward into Europe from Siberia. With typical
  sizes of several thousand kilometres, they have larger spatial scale than marine
  counterparts; the large spatial scales and timescales of several days reflect the
  slower warming and moistening of cold air over continents. Research has focused on
  the dynamics and predictability of such events; little is known about their
  thermodynamics. Estimated typical heat and moisture fluxes in outbreaks over Asia are
  below 100 W m⁻², and substantially smaller values have been reported for
  snow-covered continents. Snow reflects much of the incident solar radiation — an
  important effect in continental outbreaks reaching far outside the Arctic. Adiabatic
  warming due to downward motion in high-pressure systems can play an important role in
  the energy budget of continental cold-air outbreaks.

## Connections to the global climate system

- Mid-latitude air cools and dries in an intrusion, whereas Arctic air moistens and
  warms in a marine cold-air outbreak. These air masses typically have well-defined
  boundaries over which little lateral mixing occurs, so air-mass transformations are
  essential pathways of communication between the Arctic and mid-latitudes. In the
  global heat-engine perspective, cooling and drying of warm, moist air is the dominant
  situation in the polar branch of the mass circulation; the regionally important rapid
  warming/moistening in outbreaks is small compared to the warming and moistening of
  air in the deep tropics. When the circulation is averaged on surfaces of constant
  potential temperature, mean inflow into the Arctic occurs at heights above 700 hPa;
  the episodic and extreme intrusions carrying much of the poleward moisture flux occur
  below 850 hPa. Most Arctic air flows southward across the continents and fuels the
  baroclinic storm-track processes when it reaches the ocean; only a smaller fraction
  of Arctic air is transformed in marine cold-air outbreaks.

### Thermodynamic controls

- Assuming fixed advection, the along-wind horizontal scale of a transformation depends
  on its timescale, which largely depends on the boundary-layer and cloud processes
  discussed above. The timescale is substantially shorter for marine cold-air outbreaks
  (hours up to a day) than for continental outbreaks and intrusions (several days). The
  longer an intruding moist air mass remains in the cloudy state with substantially
  increased downward radiation, the greater its effect on the surface energy budget and
  sea ice. How the transformation timescale depends on air-mass properties, surface
  conditions, and the large-scale circulation is therefore an important research
  question.
- These thermodynamics are changing in a warming climate. Assuming fixed relative
  humidity and no major circulation change, the Clausius–Clapeyron relation implies
  more moisture injected into the Arctic in intrusions. Higher initial moisture could
  contribute to longer lifetimes of liquid clouds during the air-mass cooling,
  contributing to surface-amplified warming over the Arctic and high-latitude
  continents, and thereby to the positive Arctic lapse-rate feedback. Reduced sea-ice
  area shrinks the domain over which intruding air masses cool, increasing the relative
  importance of the cloudy state; reduced sea-ice and snow thickness increases ocean
  heat release and surface temperatures especially in the clear state — expected to
  further increase the transformation timescale.
- Arctic amplification implies that cold-air-outbreak source regions warm more than the
  regions affected by the outbreaks, reducing continental outbreak severity and
  mid-latitude cold extremes more than implied by the local increase in average winter
  temperatures. The marine outbreak source regions move poleward with the retreating
  sea-ice edge: over the North Atlantic this weakens outbreaks as their location shifts
  away from the warm Atlantic surface currents, while no major change is seen in the
  Sea of Okhotsk. Changes in ocean currents and thereby sea surface temperatures could
  additionally impact marine outbreak strength. The observed increase of open ocean
  north of Svalbard and in the northeastern Barents Sea makes these newly ice-free
  areas prone to strong heat loss and ice production during outbreaks, both leading to
  increased mixing in the ocean — contributing to the observed 'atlantification' of the
  Eurasian Basin of the Arctic Ocean. Another consequence: warmer temperatures and a
  thicker, moister boundary layer are observed over Svalbard during outbreaks. Polar
  lows in the North Atlantic are projected to become less frequent because warming
  aloft is stronger than at the surface, which suppresses their formation.
- While thermodynamic changes follow first principles, analogous dynamical theory to
  explain the interaction with large-scale dynamics is lacking — an issue related to
  the World Climate Research Program's grand challenge of clouds, circulation and
  climate sensitivity.

### Dynamical response and forcing

- In idealized simulations of Arctic air-mass formation, low-level radiative cooling
  causes a dynamic response: cold, dense outflow near the surface leads to convergence
  into the air mass aloft, balanced by downward motion in the centre — high pressure in
  the centre and the development of anticyclonic motion, in agreement with observations
  that cold, dry air tends to be associated with higher surface pressure. In numerical
  experiments without subsidence, cloudy layers in intrusions rise rapidly by
  entrainment, while observed clouds remain much closer to the surface. Downward motion
  above the clouds could maintain clouds at low levels while they entrain moist air
  from aloft. Real-world intrusions are driven by a pressure dipole (cyclone west,
  anticyclone east; Fig. 1); reanalysis composites suggest upward motion initially
  dominates on the cyclonic side of the dipole, with subsidence prevailing after about
  five days, when the air mass has largely crossed the Arctic. Tropopause polar
  vortices also generate ascent and descent over different areas and are generally
  associated with cold tropospheric anomalies. The evolution of large-scale vertical
  motion during an intrusion is a key observational challenge for both process
  understanding and model improvement.
- The strong meridional advection patterns driving intrusions and outbreaks are often
  associated with blocking-like high-pressure systems that can remain stagnant for
  several days. Mid-latitude cyclones also play an important role, but often do not
  enter the Arctic. Blocking events enhancing the meridional flow may have precursors
  in the stratosphere or far from the region of interest: if the strong zonal winds in
  the stratospheric polar vortex weaken or reverse in sudden stratospheric warming
  events, strong meridional advection patterns become more likely in the troposphere.
  Intrusions often follow Rossby-wave breaking events, which cause a local reversal of
  the climatological temperature gradient, disturb the westerly flow, and can lead to
  blocking and stronger meridional transport. Localized tropical convection over the
  Pacific Ocean can generate planetary-scale Rossby-wave trains that propagate
  poleward, where they interact with the climatological stationary waves to generate
  meridional flow and moisture advection into the Arctic.
- If disturbances of the westerlies at high latitudes became more or less frequent or
  persistent in a warming climate, this would change the frequency or characteristics
  of intrusions and outbreaks, with substantial impacts on weather and climate. To what
  extent, and by what mechanisms, Arctic amplification could lead to an increased
  frequency of such disturbances is still under discussion.

## The pathway forward

- Improved understanding of Arctic air-mass transformations and their role in the
  climate system requires a new holistic and coupled view incorporating both
  observations and models. Transformations are more than a flux of heat or moisture
  to/from the Arctic: their rich multi-scale physics provides an opportunity to better
  understand both local-scale processes (e.g. cloud microphysics, boundary-layer
  turbulence) and the feedbacks between these processes and larger-scale circulations,
  as well as coupling among atmosphere, ocean and sea ice. Model failures to adequately
  represent important aspects of the transformations impede understanding of the Arctic
  and global systems in a changing climate.
- Most of the observational basis, especially at process level, was developed in an
  Eulerian framework, but air-mass transformation occurs over Lagrangian pathways — an
  alternative conceptual thinking is required when planning observations and improving
  models that better target transformation processes across surface-temperature
  gradients, coastlines, or the marginal ice zone. The concept was pioneered for
  stratocumulus-to-cumulus transitions (ASTEX).
- The Lagrangian perspective can be applied to existing observations to improve
  understanding of, for example, spatial variation in processes and the timescales
  involved. Using trajectory analysis, cases can be identified that leverage
  consecutive observations from several locations along an air-mass trajectory,
  characterizing up- and down-stream conditions during transformation events. Key
  examples: combining icebreaker-based observations from within the icepack (SHEBA,
  ASCOS, PASCAL; see Table 1) with coastal land-based observatories, ideally with
  supporting aircraft observations (FIRE-ACE, AMISA, ACLOUD) providing spatial linking
  and context.

- The ongoing Polar Prediction Project (PPP, 2013-2022) and the Year of Polar
  Prediction (YOPP) provide additional observational, model, and coupled reanalysis
  data to the community. MOSAiC will offer a full-year perspective on the Arctic
  atmosphere and coupled climate system from the central Arctic sea ice, directly
  within a major transport pathway for intrusions; at the same time, COMBLE will focus
  on cold-air outbreaks in the North Atlantic Arctic. Advances in observatories at
  locations such as Ny Ålesund and Barrow provide additional continuous atmospheric
  profiling along important transport pathways. Though many of these observations are
  still primarily Eulerian in concept, there are new opportunities to engage mobile
  observation systems that can target and follow air masses as they move into or out of
  the Arctic: long-range aircraft systems such as HALO and HIAPER, along with other key
  airborne assets, can play a central role in linking fixed observatories. Autonomous
  aircraft (the Global Hawk, drones, airships) offer the ability to track air masses as
  they evolve and to provide near-instantaneous transects along and across the primary
  direction of the large-scale flow. Satellite products provide larger areal coverage,
  and the data can be organized along calculated trajectories; new satellite
  opportunities (e.g. EarthCARE) will allow enhanced observation of the spatial
  structure of transformation events, including insight into the associated clouds.
- Correct modelling of air-mass transformations is essential to improve forecast
  capabilities and to represent feedback mechanisms important for climate change.
  Particular foci: how models handle transitions between cloudy and cloud-free states,
  the evolution of cloud phase partitioning, and air-mass moisture sources and sinks
  (that is, precipitation efficiency) — all of which impact the spatio-temporal scales
  of transformations. The representation of exchange processes between the transforming
  air and the underlying surface also requires improvement, considering complexities
  related to strong contrasts in, for example, temperature, roughness and albedo
  between land, variable sea-ice cover, and open ocean. It is essential to employ a
  hierarchy of model perspectives — from tools resolving boundary-layer turbulent
  eddies and cloud-scale processes up to regional and global-scale models representing
  large-scale forcing and responses. In the rapidly changing Arctic it is important to
  develop models within a coupled framework: omitting surface interactions in model
  development, by forcing sub-models with observations or reanalysis, leads to severe
  biases when models are ultimately coupled.
- Suggested path forward: focus on advancing the understanding of processes important
  for air-mass transformation; with the additional observational efforts, now is an
  opportune time for the broader community to implement new knowledge in models. This
  could be supported by process-oriented model intercomparisons (cf. Larcform 1,
  ref. 33) focusing on air-mass water budgets, cloud longevity, surface energy budgets,
  entrainment and turbulence, and cloud/air-mass structural organization for both
  cold-air outbreaks and warm-air intrusions.

## Essential lessons

Air masses exchanged between lower latitudes and the Arctic undergo key
transformations involving the boundary layer, clouds, and thermodynamic and dynamical
interactions with the larger-scale environment. The transformations occur over hours
to days and across hundreds to thousands of kilometres and are unique at high
latitudes. Their thermodynamics are affected by climate change and its Arctic
amplification, and affect Arctic climate feedbacks. Current models do not adequately
represent the underlying processes, and classical fixed-in-space observations provide
only one snapshot of an ongoing transformation. Observations and model experiments
that follow air masses on their paths to and from the Arctic will substantially
advance understanding of the transformation processes.
