# AGU 2026 Plain Language Summary

In Arctic winter, the atmosphere near the surface can exist in two states: a cloudy state where clouds trap heat and keep the surface warmer, and a clear state where heat escapes and the surface becomes very cold. Many computer simulations fail to produce these clouds, leading to errors in Arctic temperature predictions.

To understand why, we tested a simulation of cold air moving over Arctic sea ice. In the simulation, ice crystals grow by pulling moisture directly from the air. When this growth happens at a fixed rate, ice crystals consume all available moisture before cloud droplets can form, and no liquid clouds ever appear. This holds across a wide range of growth rates. Only when the growth rate varies with temperature, slowing when fewer ice-forming particles are present, does our simulation produce realistic clouds containing both liquid droplets and ice crystals.

Our results show that tracking liquid water and ice as separate quantities is not enough to produce Arctic clouds. How fast ice crystals grow, and how that rate responds to local conditions, is a key controlling factor. Getting these clouds right matters for understanding Arctic warming and improving climate predictions.
